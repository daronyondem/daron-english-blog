There's a quiet conversation happening across HR and employment-law circles right now, and most people working in tech haven't caught it yet.

It goes like this. A senior employee critiques a colleague's writing in a public meeting. The critique arrives bundled, delivered as one package: "this is AI-generated," "this isn't how we write here," and "the substance is wrong." Senior witnesses nod. The colleague, three months in, takes the hit publicly and absorbs it.

A week later, HR reviews the incident — or doesn't — and notices the critic's targets cluster. Non-native English speakers. Neurodivergent employees using AI as a writing accommodation. Employees with disabilities relying on AI tooling. Newer hires who haven't yet absorbed the company's insider voice. The pattern isn't always visible from one incident. It always is from the file.

That's the conversation HR and employment lawyers are starting to have. Companies that figure out how to manage it will save themselves a generation of disparate-impact litigation. Companies that don't will spend the next five years in it.

## The Bundle

What makes the AI accusation different from ordinary peer review is that it almost never arrives alone. It comes bundled with two other claims: a voice critique ("this isn't our register," "this doesn't sound like one of us") and a substance critique ("the content is also wrong").

Each of those, taken alone, is legitimate workplace conversation. Bundled together, they create an attack vector that is almost impossible to defend in real time.

If the target defends the substance, the AI accusation undermines their credibility — the implicit logic being that they didn't really write it, so they don't really know it. If they defend their authorship, the voice critique anchors them as outside the in-group. If they defend their voice, the substance critique resurfaces.

The bundle is the mechanism. Pulling the three claims apart is the only defensive move that has a chance, and most people, taken by surprise in a public setting, do not know to do it.

## Who Gets Hit, and Why

The four categories that take this bundle most often share one thing in common: they produce written output that looks slightly different from the dominant in-group's output, for reasons that have nothing to do with AI.

**Non-native English speakers** learn formal English through instruction rather than immersion. Vocabulary from textbooks. Sentence structure from grammar drills. Register from formal correspondence. Their natural professional register skews polished and slightly formal — and AI also produces polished, slightly formal output. To an ear trained on native casual English, both sound similar for the same reason: neither carries the irregularities of native casual register. The "AI detector," biological or algorithmic, confuses the two.

**Neurodivergent employees** — particularly those with ADHD, autism-spectrum profiles, or executive-function differences — often use AI as a writing accommodation. Drafting is the part of writing that is hardest for some neurodivergent profiles. AI handles drafting; the human supplies substance and editing. This is a legitimate accessibility use, parallel to voice-to-text or grammar-correction tools. It is also indistinguishable from "AI-authored" content to a casual reader.

**Employees with disabilities** for whom AI tooling is functional accommodation — visual impairments using AI summarization, motor impairments using AI drafting, cognitive disabilities using AI restructuring — face the same pattern.

**Newer hires** in their first year haven't yet absorbed insider voice. Insider voice is the accumulation of micro-conventions: what gets said directly, what gets implied, which aphorisms are in-bounds, which terminology is reserved for senior leadership, what tone you use writing to the leadership team. These conventions aren't documented anywhere. They're learned by osmosis. A new hire writing well-structured, factually accurate work in their natural professional register can land as "off" because they haven't yet picked up the camouflage.

In each case, what looks like an "AI-generated tell" is actually a non-AI signal — language origin, neurology, disability, tenure — being misread.

## Register Policing

We need a name for what's happening, because "tone policing" is taken and doesn't quite fit.

Tone policing critiques *emotional content*: "you sound angry," "calm down," "be more constructive."

What's happening here critiques *register*: formality level, vocabulary choice, sentence structure, density of aphorism, which professional voice you sound like. It is invisible to the in-group because the in-group does not notice it is enforcing anything. They experience their own register as normal and any deviation as off.

I will call it **register policing**: critiquing colleagues' professional output not on substance or accuracy, but on whether their formality, vocabulary, and rhetorical structure match the in-group's transmitted norms.

Register policing predates AI. AI tools made it visible because AI broadened the population producing polished written output, and that population now includes more outsiders than the in-group is comfortable with.

Two features make register policing particularly hard to engage on its own terms. First, it is unfalsifiable. There is no rulebook. No published style guide codifies "we don't open paragraphs with conditional clauses" or "we don't use the word *regime*." The norms transmit by inheritance and enforce by senior practitioners. Second, the critique inherits the social weight of consensus — *everyone knows* — without ever requiring anyone to write down what everyone supposedly knows.

When unfalsifiability meets the bundle, the result is a critique that cannot be objectively disproved, is delivered with the social weight of common knowledge, and disproportionately targets categories of people who were already underrepresented in the dominant register. That is the textbook structure of cultural credentialism.

## Why This Is a Disparate-Impact Problem

US employment law — Title VII for national origin, the ADA for disability, and various jurisdictional protections — does not require intent. It requires impact.

If a company's senior employees disproportionately critique non-native English speakers' writing as "AI-generated" or "not our voice," and those critiques materially affect performance reviews, promotion velocity, or scope assignment, that is a disparate-impact pattern, regardless of whether anyone is acting in bad faith.

Three signals move this from theoretical to legally actionable:

1. A documented pattern of who receives the critique — the file HR builds, whether they intend to or not.
2. Material consequence on employment terms — review language, promotion decisions, project assignments.
3. The absence of a published policy distinguishing acceptable AI tool use from unacceptable AI authorship.

Most companies have none of those policies, are not tracking the pattern, and have senior populations who treat AI accusations as legitimate craft critique rather than a systemic question requiring management.

## A Leadership Question, Not a Tooling Question

The instinct in most organizations is to treat this as an AI-policy problem. It is not. AI tools are changing *who* can produce polished output. Existing culture's reaction is to tighten *who is allowed to*. That is not a tooling failure mode. It is a leadership and organizational-design failure mode.

In *Agentic Organizations*, the framework I write toward is the CCD Triad — Culture, Communication, and Delegation as the three interfaces organizations need to redesign as AI capacity broadens. Register policing sits squarely at the intersection of Culture and Communication. AI broadens communication capacity. Existing culture responds by enforcing tighter membership criteria. The healthy adaptation is to update the cultural-communication interface for the new capacity. The unhealthy adaptation is to use cultural enforcement as a substitute for adaptation.

The leadership move is to design the interface explicitly, rather than letting it default to whoever is loudest in the meeting.

## What Organizations Should Do

Three concrete moves.

**Publish an actual AI-use policy.** Not a ban. Not a free-for-all. A written document that distinguishes acceptable AI-assisted drafting from unacceptable AI-authored output. The substance is the human's. The prose can be assisted. The human is responsible for both. Most companies don't have this. The absence is itself the disparate-impact exposure, because it leaves the line between acceptable and unacceptable to be enforced by whoever critiques most aggressively in meetings.

**Treat AI-accusation patterns the way you treat dress-code disparate-impact patterns.** When senior employees disproportionately critique a protected category's work for AI authorship, voice mismatch, or fit, that is the same compliance question as when senior employees disproportionately enforce dress code on women, on Black employees, on Muslim employees. The legal frame is identical. The HR posture should be identical. Most HR organizations apply the second frame and have not yet noticed the first is the same shape.

**Distinguish substance critique from voice critique in performance review language.** "The technical claim on page four is wrong" is a substance critique. "This doesn't sound like us" is a voice critique. Voice critique should not appear in performance review language. If it does, it should be flagged as a process exception requiring a documented standard. The absence of that distinction is the operational mechanism by which register policing becomes career-affecting.

## What Individuals Should Do

If you are on the receiving end of an AI-accusation bundle, three moves matter.

**Don't argue the bundle on its terms.** Pull it apart. The AI claim, the voice claim, and the substance claim are three separate questions. Defending all three simultaneously is the trap. Answer them one at a time, on the record, in writing where you can.

**Document silently.** Save the comments, the timing, the witnesses, who else has been targeted with the same vocabulary. Do not surface the file during an active critique cycle. Surfacing it then reads as retaliation regardless of merit. Build the file. Keep the option live.

**Know the legal terrain.** If a pattern recurs and materially affects your employment terms, the appropriate channel is HR or an employment attorney, not the manager chain. That decision tree is separate from the in-the-moment defense, and conflating the two weakens both.

## A Closing Note

I am writing this because I have watched it happen, and I have felt it. The non-native English speaker reading their own polished output and being told it cannot be theirs. The neurodivergent colleague defending an accommodation as a craft choice. The new hire absorbing a public hit because they have not yet learned which words are out-of-bounds in their organization.

The pattern is not theoretical, and it is not specific to any one company. It is becoming the way insider/outsider sorting happens in organizations where AI tools have outpaced the cultural infrastructure to govern them.

Register policing is not a slip in the craft conversation. It is a leadership question, and the organizations that take it seriously will keep the people the rest are about to lose.
